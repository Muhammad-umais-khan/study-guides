1. Traditional and Agile Process Models Context & Comparison

This comparison shows how modern Agile and Scrum differ from traditional plan-driven models and when each approach may be appropriate.

Old Models vs. New Models: What Each Is Good For

Model / Approach What It Was Good For (Strengths) Main Weakness (Why It Struggled) How New Models (Agile / Scrum) Improve It
Waterfall & Iterative Waterfall
(Royce, 1970s • Linear / Loop-back)		 Small, stable projects with fixed requirements, clear upfront budgets, and heavy documentation needs (team turnover doesn't break the project). Rigid to change; working software and testing happen only at the very end; backward loops in iterative waterfall are slow and costly. Delivers working software every 1–4 weeks (Sprint) and welcomes changing requirements at any stage without costly contract renegotiations.
V-Model
(Verification & Validation extension)		 Safety-critical or strict-compliance projects needing early test planning (test cases created at every SDLC stage to prevent bias). Still linear and plan-driven; requires heavy upfront documentation and more planning work; struggles when user requirements evolve mid-project. Replaces single end-of-project validation with continuous Inspection (Daily Scrum, Sprint Review) and Adaptation every Sprint.
Prototyping
(Throwaway/Reference vs. Evolutionary/Foundation)		 Clarifying unclear UI/UX or unknown requirements early using Low, Medium, or High fidelity mockups before full-scale production. Throwaway prototypes get discarded (extra cost); evolutionary prototypes risk poor architecture if rushed without discipline. Every Scrum Increment acts like a real, production-quality evolutionary slice reviewed directly by stakeholders in the Sprint Review.
Incremental (Staged & Parallel)
(Module/time grouping & concurrent teams)		 Staged IDM: delivering modules in timed stages. Parallel / Subsystem Construction: overlapping spec/dev/val or splitting subsystems across teams to cut calendar time. Plan-driven incremental still tries to identify all increments upfront; parallel teams still rely on rigid upfront architecture specs. Agile Incremental (Scrum): only top-priority items are detailed upfront; the Product Backlog stays emergent and adapts after every Sprint.
Integration & Configuration
(Reuse-oriented / COTS)		 Fast, lower-cost delivery by assembling tested components (Integration = connecting modules) and tuning generic apps (Configuration = adapting to user reqs). Requirements often have to bend to fit what the COTS software offers; vendor lock-in and interface mismatches. Agile teams still reuse libraries/APIs inside Sprints, but keep user value and continuous customer collaboration in control rather than vendor limits.

Key Process Model Terms

  • Plan-Driven (Predictive) vs. Evolutionary (Adaptive): Plan-driven plans everything upfront assuming stable requirements (Waterfall, V-Model); Evolutionary expects change and adapts continuously (Prototyping, Agile, Scrum).
  • Verification vs. Validation: Verification checks whether the product conforms to its specifications (static analysis, such as SRS, design, and code reviews). Validation checks whether the product satisfies user needs (dynamic analysis by executing the software).
  • Stage vs. Increment: A stage is a step in a cycle (or module/time grouping in Staged IDM); an increment is a delivered slice of working functionality.

Why Plan-Driven Approaches Struggle with Change

Five structural weaknesses drove the shift to Agile:

  1. Significant overhead from comprehensive planning and documentation.
  2. Overhead dominates actual development and testing in small & medium projects.
  3. No flexibility to adapt when requirements change.
  4. Late demonstrations of working software to users.
  5. Process bureaucracy and rigid contract orientation.

2. Agility & The Agile Manifesto (2001) Core Concepts

Core Definitions

  • Agility (Literal meaning: Swift): The ability to respond rapidly and effectively to change; a way to succeed in uncertain and turbulent environments by understanding what is happening today, identifying uncertainty, and adapting as you go.
  • Agile Software Development: An umbrella term for frameworks and practices (such as Scrum, XP, and Kanban) built on the Agile Manifesto's 4 values and 12 principles. Above all, Agile is a mindset: face uncertainty by trying something small, getting feedback, and adjusting.

The Four Values of the Agile Manifesto (2001)

We Value (Left Side — Higher Priority)Over (Right Side — Lower Priority)Meaning
1. Individuals and interactionsProcesses and toolsTeam communication principle: skilled people talking directly solve problems faster than rigid tools.
2. Working softwareComprehensive documentationReal, running functionality proves actual progress far better than hundreds of pages of specs.
3. Customer collaborationContract negotiationWork alongside the customer continuously instead of arguing over rigid upfront contract clauses.
4. Responding to changeFollowing a planAdapt quickly when business priorities shift rather than blindly following an outdated plan.

Common Misconception About the Agile Values

The Manifesto says "while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more." Agile does NOT mean zero documentation, zero planning, or zero tools—it means lightweight, just-enough documentation and adaptable plans.

3. Scrum Framework: Origin, Theory, Pillars & Values Theory

History & Rugby Analogy ("Scrummage")

  • Creators & 1990s Origin: Developed in the early 1990s by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, predating the formal publication of the Agile Manifesto (2001). Alongside Extreme Programming (XP), its creators recognized their iterative, people-centered philosophy differed from traditional waterfall processes and co-authored the Agile Manifesto.
  • Origin of the Word "Scrum": Short for "scrummage", a rugby term used to restart play after a minor rule infringement—where players pack closely together to gain possession of the ball and push forward as one unified unit.
  • 1986 Harvard Business Review Paper: Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka introduced this rugby analogy to product development in 1986 to describe a cross-functional team working tightly together to incrementally move a project forward.

Formal Definitions of Scrum

  1. Lightweight Framework: A low-overhead framework that helps people, teams, and organizations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems under the umbrella of Agile software development.
  2. Agile Project Management Framework: A framework based on an incremental approach to development that helps teams structure and manage their work through a set of values and practices (both Scrum's own and broader Agile practices).
  3. Teamwork, Accountability & Iterative Progress: A framework emphasizing teamwork, accountability, and evolutionary/iterative progress (iterating across the entire software product rather than building isolated phases) toward a well-defined goal.
  4. Process Flexibility (Scrum vs. XP): Scrum is an Agile-based framework that does not mandate specific software engineering or coding practices (such as pair programming or test-first development / TDD), giving teams high technical flexibility—unlike Extreme Programming (XP), which prescribes strict engineering rules.

Core Empirical Premise: "Start with what can be seen or known. After that, track the progress and tweak as necessary."

Complete Personal Definition of Scrum

Scrum is a lightweight Agile project management framework that helps people, teams and organizations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems. It is based on an incremental approach to development and on empiricism (start with what can be seen or known, then track progress and adapt as necessary), and it structures teamwork through a set of values and practices that emphasize teamwork, accountability and iterative progress of the whole product, rather than isolated phases, toward a well-defined goal. Unlike Extreme Programming (XP), Scrum does not mandate specific engineering or coding practices, such as pair programming or test-driven development, so teams keep technical flexibility.

Scrum Theory

1. Empiricism & Lean Thinking

Empiricism: Making decisions based on real-world observation and experience rather than guesswork.
Lean Thinking: Focusing strictly on essentials and reducing waste by avoiding extras.

2. Incremental Flow & Risk Control

Uses an incremental approach/flow to optimize predictability and control risk through continuous customer collaboration.

3. Collective Capability

Engages people who collectively possess all the skills needed to do the work and actively share those skills across the team as needed.

The Three Pillars of Scrum & Five Scrum Values

The Three Pillars of Scrum

  1. Transparency: Process and work visibility to all stakeholders—everyone performing and receiving the work shares the exact same understanding and information.
  2. Inspection: Artifacts and processes are inspected frequently to detect variances, bottlenecks, or defects early.
  3. Adaptation: Whenever inspection reveals process deviation or product variance outside acceptable limits, adjustments must be made as soon as possible to minimize further deviation.

The Five Scrum Values

  • Commitment: Personal dedication toward achieving the team's goals and supporting one another.
  • Focus: Concentrating effort on the work of the current Sprint to make steady progress toward goals.
  • Openness: Being transparent and honest about the process, ongoing work, and work challenges.
  • Respect: Mutual respect in communication, collaboration, feedback, and knowledge sharing.
  • Courage: Courage to face challenges, do the right thing, and work on tough problems.

In Practice: Guides priority-driven planning, time/effort estimation, and ranking requirements strictly by priority.

Scrum vs. Extreme Programming (XP) at a Glance

Key distinction: Scrum organizes how work is managed; XP prescribes how code is written. The two are often combined.

4. Scrum Artifacts & Requirement Quality Criteria DEEP • INVEST • CTF

Scrum Artifacts represent work or value designed to maximize transparency across the team and stakeholders.

The Three Scrum Artifacts

  1. Product Backlog: A single, centralized repository/database (not an increment backlog) containing an emergent, ordered, and prioritized list of user needs for the entire product, written as User Stories (concise, clear, not overly vague). It is never written completely in one go upfront—it fills up and evolves increment by increment.
  2. Sprint Backlog: The plan for a single increment (Sprint) containing three things: the Sprint Goal (Why), the selected items/user stories (What), and an actionable plan for delivering them (How). It is owned by the Developers, who decide who does which task, self-manage the work, and update the Sprint Backlog throughout the Sprint as they learn more.
  3. Increment: Concrete working functionality (piece of software) achieved through the work completed in a Sprint that meets quality standards and integrates seamlessly with all prior Increments.

DEEP Criteria (For a Healthy Product Backlog)

LetterCriterionWhat It Means in Practice
DDetailed AppropriatelyTop-priority user stories ready for the next Sprint have rich detail; lower-priority stories further down remain broader until needed.
EEmergentThe backlog is never frozen; new stories are added, outdated ones removed, and items refined as user feedback emerges.
EEstimatedEvery backlog item carries a relative size or effort estimate so the team can forecast delivery capacity.
PPrioritizedItems are strictly ordered by business value, risk, and dependencies so the most valuable features are built first.

INVEST vs. CTF Criteria (For Writing Good User Stories)

INVEST Criteria (6 Rules)

  • I — Independent: Self-contained with minimal dependencies so stories can be built in any order.
  • N — Negotiable: A reminder for conversation between Product Owner & Developers, not a rigid contract.
  • V — Valuable: Delivers clear, tangible value to the end user or customer.
  • E — Estimable: Clear enough for Developers to reasonably estimate implementation effort.
  • S — Small: Sized small enough to be designed, coded, and tested within a single Sprint.
  • T — Testable: Has clear acceptance criteria so completion can be objectively verified.

CTF Criteria (3-Rule Alternative)

Terminology: in this guide, CTF = Clear, Testable, Feasible. The 3 Cs (Card, Conversation, Confirmation) are a separate idea about how a story is written and verified; keep the two labels distinct.

  • C — Clear: Written in plain, unambiguous language understood identically by developers, testers, and stakeholders.
  • T — Testable: Structured with verifiable pass/fail conditions for QA testing.
  • F — Feasible: Realistic and achievable within technical constraints, resources, and Sprint timeframe.

Quick Memory Hook

DEEP = whole Product Backlog.
INVEST or CTF = individual User Stories inside the backlog.

5. The Scrum Team & Specific Roles Accountabilities

  • Cross-Functional & Self-Managing / Self-Organizing: Collectively possesses all skills needed to create value every Sprint (analysis, design, coding, testing) and decides internally who works on what and how—no external manager assigns tasks.
  • Optimal Team Size (10 or Fewer): Small enough to stay agile and communicate quickly, yet large enough to complete significant work within a Sprint.
  • End-to-End Accountability: Responsible for all product-related activities—stakeholder collaboration, verification, maintenance, operation, experimentation, and R&D.

Common Misconceptions About Scrum Roles

  • The Scrum Master is NOT a traditional project manager who assigns tasks or bosses developers around—Scrum teams are self-organizing.
  • Only the Product Owner owns and orders the Product Backlog (though Developers may help refine/estimate items).
  • The Sprint Backlog belongs to the Developers: they decide how to organize the work and adapt the plan during the Sprint. The Scrum Master supports them; neither the Scrum Master nor the Product Owner assigns tasks.
  • "Developers" in Scrum includes everyone doing the build work (coders, testers, UI/UX designers, database engineers)—not just programmers.

6. The Five Scrum Events (Ceremonies) Time-Boxed Rhythm

Scrum events are formal activities that facilitate the framework, create regularity, promote collaboration, and keep the team aligned. They enable Transparency, Inspection, and Adaptation while minimizing unscheduled meetings.

Scrum Event When & Duration Purpose & Key Activities Pillar Enabled
1. Sprint Fixed container of 1–4 weeks The overarching time-boxed heartbeat during which a usable, quality-checked Increment is built from the Sprint Backlog. A new Sprint starts immediately after the previous one ends. All Three Pillars (Container)
2. Sprint Planning Start of the Sprint Collaborative meeting to decide what work will be done (selecting top Product Backlog items), the techniques/how to build it, and who is responsible (the Developers decide who does what)—producing the Sprint Backlog. Transparency & Adaptation
3. Daily Scrum Every day (~15 minutes) Short daily sync among Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal, adapt the daily plan, and surface impediments/issues early. Inspection & Adaptation
4. Sprint Review End of the Sprint Working session to present/demonstrate completed work (Increment) to stakeholders and gather feedback to update/reprioritize the Product Backlog. Inspection & Adaptation (Product)
5. Sprint Retrospective Very end of Sprint (after Review) Internal team meeting to inspect what went well, what didn't go well, and how to improve processes, tools, and teamwork in the next Sprint. Inspection & Adaptation (Process)

Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective

Sprint Review inspects the PRODUCT (working Increment) with external stakeholders to update the Product Backlog.
Sprint Retrospective inspects the PROCESS & TEAMWORK internally (without external stakeholders) to improve how the team works in the next Sprint.

7b. Concept Connection Chains Concept Connections

These chains show cause and effect between Scrum concepts. Linking verbs such as enables, triggers, updates, feeds, and results in make the relationships explicit.

Chain 1 — Scrum Pillars → Events → Artifacts

Chain 1 diagram: each row shows the dominant link; in Scrum every pillar operates in every event.

Concept Summary (Chain 1)

Scrum’s three pillars operate through its events to update its artifacts. Transparency keeps the Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment, and Definition of Done visible. Inspection lets the Scrum Team assess progress and outcomes, while Adaptation adjusts work or process based on what inspection reveals. Scrum events provide regular opportunities for these pillars to operate, and updated artifacts make current work transparent for the next inspection.

Chain 2 — User Needs → DEEP Backlog → INVEST/CTF Stories → Sprint Increment

Chain 2 diagram: the Sprint Review feedback loops back into the Product Backlog and restarts the chain.

Concept Summary (Chain 2)

Requirements move from user needs into an ordered, emergent Product Backlog. Backlog items are refined to an appropriate level and assessed for quality using criteria such as DEEP, INVEST, or CTF. During Sprint Planning, the Product Owner and Developers establish a Sprint Goal, select work, and create the Sprint Backlog. Developers build a usable Increment that meets the Definition of Done; inspection and stakeholder feedback at the Sprint Review inform future Product Backlog updates.

Chain 3 — Agile Values → Principles → Concrete Scrum Practices

Chain 3 diagram: one row per Manifesto value.

Concept Summary (Chain 3)

The four Manifesto values are expressed through twelve principles, and Scrum applies these ideas through its framework. Responding to change is supported by an emergent Product Backlog and reprioritization at Sprint Reviews. The focus on working software is reflected in usable Increments that meet the Definition of Done. Customer collaboration is supported by the Product Owner, stakeholders, and Sprint Review feedback. The value of individuals and interactions is reflected in self-managing Developers, Scrum events, and continuous reflection and adaptation.

8. In Easy Words (Plain-English Layman Summary) Big Picture

How Everything Fits Together — Without Jargon

Traditional plan-driven models such as Waterfall and the V-Model rely on more upfront planning, while Agile emphasizes feedback and adaptation. Scrum is an Agile framework grounded in empiricism and lean thinking. Its three pillars are Transparency, Inspection, and Adaptation, supported by the values of Commitment, Focus, Openness, Respect, and Courage. The Product Owner manages the emergent, ordered Product Backlog; the Scrum Team plans and works toward a Sprint Goal within a Sprint of one month or less; and the Developers create a usable Increment that meets the Definition of Done. Sprint Reviews inspect the product and gather feedback, while Sprint Retrospectives inspect and improve the team’s way of working.
