Agility & The Agile Manifesto

Why plan-driven models failed on small/medium projects, the 4 Manifesto values, and all 12 Agile principles.

1.1 Why Plan-Driven Approaches Failed in Small & Medium Projects

Class Notes: 04-Nov-26

Five structural weaknesses drove the software industry away from heavy plan-driven models toward Agile:

  1. Significant overhead from heavy upfront planning and comprehensive documentation.
  2. Overhead dominates actual development and testing effort in small and medium-sized projects.
  3. No flexibility to adapt smoothly when customer requirements change mid-project.
  4. Late demonstrations of working software to end users (often only at the very end).
  5. Process bureaucracy and rigid contract negotiation over real collaboration.

1.2 Core Definitions: Agility & Agile Software Development

Definition
  • Agility (Literal meaning: Swift): The ability to respond rapidly and effectively to change; a way to succeed in uncertain and turbulent environments by understanding what is happening today, identifying uncertainty, and adapting as you go.
  • Agile Software Development: An umbrella term for lightweight frameworks and practices (such as Scrum, Extreme Programming [XP], and Kanban) built on the Agile Manifesto's 4 values and 12 principles. Above all, Agile is a mindset: face uncertainty by delivering something small, getting feedback, and adjusting.

1.3 The Four Values of the Agile Manifesto (2001)

We can further understand each value by using an example of a university canteen food-ordering app. First, individuals and interactions come before processes and tools: instead of raising a formal ticket and waiting days for approval, the developers spend ten minutes face to face with the canteen manager agreeing how combo deals are priced. Progress is judged by runnable features, so the team demonstrates a working digital menu and order cart at the end of its first two-week Sprint instead of delivering a 150-page specification; this is the primary measure of progress (Principle 7). Documentation is not abandoned, since the team keeps short user stories and acceptance tests, but only as much as is useful. Third, customer collaboration comes before contract negotiation. The canteen manager works alongside the developers, tests each release and suggests interface changes, rather than signing a fixed contract that freezes every screen. This continuous dialogue supports Principles 1 and 4, early and continuous delivery and daily work between business people and developers.

Finally, responding to change comes before following a plan. When the university mandates mobile-wallet payment midway through the semester, the team welcomes the change (Principle 2), talks directly with the manager, demonstrates the working ordering feature, and reprioritizes the Product Backlog rather than defending an outdated plan. Agility, therefore, is not the absence of process or documentation but a readiness to change direction while keeping all four values in balance.

Scrum Framework: Origin, Theory, Pillars & Values

1986 rugby analogy, the 4 formal definitions of Scrum, Empiricism & Lean Thinking, the 3 Pillars, 5 Values, and Scrum vs. XP.

2.1 History & Rugby Analogy (“Scrummage”)

  • Creators & 1990s Origin: Developed in the early 1990s by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, predating the Agile Manifesto (2001). Alongside Extreme Programming (XP), its creators recognized their iterative, people-centered philosophy differed fundamentally from traditional waterfall processes and co-authored the Agile Manifesto.
  • Origin of the Word “Scrum”: Short for “scrummage”, a rugby term used to restart play after a minor rule infringement—where players pack closely together to gain possession of the ball and push forward together as a single unit.
  • 1986 Harvard Business Review Paper: Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka introduced this rugby analogy to product development in their landmark 1986 Harvard Business Review paper, describing a cross-functional team working tightly together to incrementally move a project forward.

2.2 Four Formal Definitions of Scrum (Lecture Slides)

Core Definitions
  1. Lightweight Framework: A low-overhead framework that helps people, teams, and organizations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems under the umbrella of Agile software development.
  2. Agile Project Management Framework: A framework based on an incremental and iterative approach to development that helps teams structure and manage their work through a set of values and practices (both Scrum's own practices and broader Agile principles).
  3. Teamwork, Accountability & Iterative Progress: A project management framework emphasizing teamwork, accountability, and evolutionary/iterative progress (iterating across the entire software product rather than building isolated phases) toward a well-defined goal.
  4. Process Flexibility (Scrum vs. XP): Scrum is an Agile-based framework that does not mandate specific software engineering or coding practices (such as pair programming or test-first development / TDD), giving teams high technical flexibility—unlike Extreme Programming (XP), which prescribes strict engineering practices.
Core Empirical Premise Empiricism ⇒ Observe What Is Known → Track Progress → Adapt Immediately

2.3 Scrum Theory, Three Pillars & Five Values

1. Empiricism & Lean Thinking

Empiricism: Decisions come from real-world observation and experience rather than guesswork.
Lean Thinking: Focuses strictly on essentials and reduces waste by avoiding extras.

2. Incremental Flow

Uses an incremental approach/flow to optimize predictability and control risk through continuous customer collaboration.

3. Collective Capability

Engages people who collectively possess all skills needed to do the work and actively share those skills across the team as needed.

Memorize • 3 Pillars

The Three Pillars of Scrum

  1. Transparency: Process and work visibility to all stakeholders—everyone performing and receiving the work has access to the exact same information and shared understanding.
  2. Inspections: Artifacts generated and processes used/executed must be inspected frequently and diligently to detect variances, bottlenecks, or defects early.
  3. Adaptation: Whenever inspection reveals process deviation or product variance outside acceptable limits, adjustments must be made as soon as possible to minimize further deviation.
Memorize • 5 Values

The Five Scrum Values

  • Commitment: Personal dedication toward achieving the team's goals and supporting one another.
  • Focus: Concentrating effort on the work of the current Sprint to make steady progress toward goals.
  • Openness: Being transparent and honest about the process, ongoing work, and work challenges.
  • Respect: Mutual respect in communication, collaboration, feedback, and knowledge sharing.
  • Courage: Courage to face challenges, do the right thing, and work on tough problems.

Guides priority-driven planning, time/effort estimation, and ranking requirements strictly by priority.

Same topic, different answer depth: define/list for 1–2 marks; explain how the concepts work together for 3–4.

1–2 Marks | Define / List

Scrum is a lightweight framework for generating value through adaptive solutions to complex problems. It rests on empiricism (decisions based on observation and experience) and lean thinking (focus on essentials and reduce waste). Its pillars are Transparency, Inspection, Adaptation; its values are Commitment, Focus, Openness, Respect, Courage.

3–4 Marks | Explain / Describe

Scrum puts empiricism and lean thinking into practice through its three pillars. Empiricism means decisions rest on observed evidence rather than guesswork, and lean thinking means focusing on essentials and avoiding waste. We can further understand how the pillars work by using an example of a hospital outpatient appointment app. First, Transparency makes the Product Backlog and Sprint Backlog visible to the Scrum Team, hospital administrators and clinic staff, and the shared Definition of Done ensures everyone agrees what “finished” means, so nobody works from a private assumption. Second, Inspection depends on that visibility: at the 15-minute Daily Scrum the Developers notice that the doctor-schedule API returns duplicate time slots, and at the Sprint Review the administrators inspect the working Increment and realize patients also need SMS reminders.

Third, Adaptation responds to what inspection reveals. The Developers adjust the Sprint Backlog straight after the Daily Scrum by adding a task to fix the API, and after the Review the Product Owner moves the SMS-reminder story to the top of the Product Backlog so it can be planned for the next Sprint. The five Scrum values keep this cycle working in practice: Commitment keeps the team dedicated to the Sprint Goal, Focus keeps Developers on Sprint work rather than side tasks, Openness means the API problem is raised early rather than hidden, Respect lets stakeholders criticize the Increment constructively, and Courage lets the team tackle the difficult scheduling logic. The distinction matters for the mark: the three pillars describe how work is made visible and adjusted, while the five values describe how people behave while doing it.

Mark-loser warning: Keep the three pillars distinct from the five values, and define empiricism as evidence from observation—not guessing.

Scrum Artifacts & Quality Criteria (DEEP, INVEST & CTF)

The three Scrum artifacts with their commitments, plus how DEEP evaluates the Product Backlog while INVEST/CTF evaluates User Stories.

3.1 The Three Scrum Artifacts (and Their Commitments)

3 Artifacts + Commitments
  1. Product Backlog (Commitment: Product Goal): A single, centralized repository/database containing an emergent, ordered, and prioritized list of user needs for the entire product (not just a single increment), written as User Stories. It evolves increment by increment.
  2. Sprint Backlog (Commitment: Sprint Goal): The plan for a single Sprint consisting of three parts: the Sprint Goal (Why), the selected items/user stories (What), and an actionable plan for delivering them (How & Who). Owned and updated by the Developers throughout the Sprint.
  3. Increment (Commitment: Definition of Done): Concrete working functionality (piece of software) completed in a Sprint that meets the shared quality standards (Definition of Done) and integrates seamlessly with all prior Increments.

3.2 DEEP Criteria (For a Healthy Product Backlog)

LetterCriterionWhat It Means in Practice
DDetailed AppropriatelyTop-priority user stories ready for the next Sprint have rich detail; lower-priority items further down remain broader until needed.
EEmergentThe backlog is never frozen; new stories are added, outdated ones removed, and items refined as user feedback emerges.
EEstimatedEvery backlog item carries a relative size or effort estimate so the team can forecast Sprint capacity.
PPrioritizedItems are strictly ordered by business value, risk, and dependencies so the most valuable features are built first.

3.3 INVEST vs. CTF Criteria (For Writing Good User Stories)

INVEST Criteria (6 Rules)

  • I — Independent: Self-contained with minimal dependencies so stories can be built in any order.
  • N — Negotiable: A reminder for conversation between Product Owner & Developers, not a rigid contract.
  • V — Valuable: Delivers clear, tangible value to the end user or customer.
  • E — Estimable: Clear enough for Developers to reasonably estimate implementation effort.
  • S — Small: Sized small enough to be designed, coded, and tested within a single Sprint.
  • T — Testable: Has clear acceptance criteria so completion can be objectively verified.

CTF Criteria (3-Rule Alternative)

Slide rule: CTF = Clear, Testable, Feasible (do not confuse with the 3 Cs: Card, Conversation, Confirmation).

  • C — Clear: Written in plain, unambiguous language understood identically by developers, testers, and stakeholders.
  • T — Testable: Structured with verifiable pass/fail conditions for QA testing.
  • F — Feasible: Realistic and achievable within technical constraints, resources, and Sprint timeframe.
Scope Rule

DEEP evaluates the whole Product Backlog.
INVEST or CTF evaluates individual User Stories.

Dual-Format Answer Box: Artifacts, DEEP, INVEST & CTF

Same topic, different answer depth: define/list the terms for 1–2 marks; explain their scope and use in a scenario for 3–4.

1–2 Marks | Define / List

Product Backlog: emergent, ordered list of product needs; commitment is the Product Goal. Sprint Backlog: Sprint Goal, selected items and actionable plan. Increment: usable work meeting the Definition of Done. DEEP assesses the backlog (Detailed appropriately, Emergent, Estimated, Prioritized); INVEST assesses stories (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable); CTF means Clear, Testable, Feasible.

3–4 Marks | Explain / Describe

In a campus bookstore app, the three Scrum artifacts form a chain of commitments. The Product Backlog is one emergent, ordered list of everything the product may need, committed to the Product Goal, and the Product Owner keeps it current as students request features. The Sprint Backlog is the plan for a single Sprint, made of the Sprint Goal (why), the selected items (what) and an actionable plan (how), committed to the Sprint Goal and owned by the Developers. The Increment is the usable working software completed in that Sprint, committed to the Definition of Done. Quality is then checked at two different scopes.

To judge the health of the whole backlog, we use DEEP. The top item, “search textbooks by course”, is Detailed appropriately for the upcoming Sprint while distant items stay broad; the backlog remains Emergent as requests arrive and outdated items are removed; every item is Estimated so capacity can be forecast; and the list is Prioritized by value, risk and dependencies. To judge a single story, we use INVEST and CTF. The story “As a student, I want to filter books by course so that I can find required texts” is Independent of the checkout feature, Negotiable in its filter layout, Valuable to students, Estimable by the Developers, Small enough for one Sprint and Testable against clear acceptance criteria. CTF checks the same story is Clear, Testable and Feasible within the Sprint timeframe.

The story then moves through the artifacts. The Developers select it during Sprint Planning, add it to the Sprint Backlog with the tasks needed to build it, and build and test it during the Sprint. It counts as an Increment only if it satisfies the Definition of Done, for example code reviewed, tests passing and documentation updated, and that Increment is then shown at the Sprint Review. For marks, remember that DEEP judges the backlog as a whole, while INVEST and CTF judge individual stories.

Mark-loser warning: DEEP evaluates the whole Product Backlog; INVEST and CTF evaluate individual user stories.

Scrum Team, Specific Roles & The Five Events

Cross-functional self-management (≤ 10 members), Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers, and the 5 time-boxed Scrum events.

4.1 Scrum Team Structure & The Three Specific Roles

  • Cross-Functional & Self-Managing / Self-Organizing: Collectively possesses all skills needed to create value every Sprint (analysis, design, coding, testing) and decides internally who works on what and how—no external manager assigns tasks.
  • Optimal Team Size (≤ 10 Members): Small enough to remain agile and communicate quickly, yet large enough to complete significant work within a Sprint.
  • End-to-End Accountability: Accountable for all product-related activities—stakeholder collaboration, verification, maintenance, operation, experimentation, and R&D.

4.2 The Five Scrum Events (Ceremonies)

What is a Scrum Event? A formal, time-boxed activity designed to facilitate the Scrum process, create regularity, promote collaboration, and keep the team aligned—enabling Transparency, Inspection, and Adaptation while minimizing unscheduled meetings.

Scrum Event When & Duration Purpose & Key Activities Pillar Enabled
1. Sprint Fixed container of 1–4 weeks The overarching time-boxed heartbeat during which a usable, quality-checked Increment is built from the Sprint Backlog. A new Sprint starts immediately after the previous one ends. All Three Pillars (Container)
2. Sprint Planning Start of the Sprint Collaborative meeting to decide what work will be done (selecting top Product Backlog items), the techniques/how to build it, and who is responsible (Developers decide who does what)—producing the Sprint Backlog. Transparency & Adaptation

The Sprint is the container for everything. Requirements Engineering (RE)

“A process within a process”—abstraction levels, Functional vs. Non-Functional (Dependability) vs. Domain requirements, the 5 RE stages, and Plan-Driven vs. Agile RE.

6.1 Core Definition & RE Architecture Diagram

Definition

Requirements Engineering (RE) is described as “a process within a process”—the systematic set of activities used to discover, analyze, document, validate, and maintain the services that a customer requires from a software system and the constraints under which it operates and is developed. RE acts as a bridge between the user and the developer, translating needs into requirements that can be built and checked.

Customers and end users may differ: a customer may purchase or sponsor the system, while end users operate it (for example, a hospital administrator commissions an appointment system used by patients and receptionists).

6.2 Three Abstraction Levels of Requirements

LevelDefinition & AudienceConcrete Example (University Bus App)
1. Business RequirementsHigh-level statements of organizational goals, strategic objectives, and why the software is being built (for sponsors & executives).Reduce student bus-boarding delays by 40% and eliminate paper bus-pass printing costs.
2. User RequirementsStatements in natural language plus simple diagrams describing what services the system provides to end users and operational constraints (for customers & managers).A student shall be able to view the live location of campus buses on their route map.
3. System RequirementsStructured, detailed technical specifications setting out exact system functions, data formats, and constraints (serves as the developer/tester contract in the SRS).The mobile client shall poll the GPS telemetry endpoint every 5 seconds via HTTPS and render coordinates within 300 ms.

6.3 System Requirement Types: FR, NFR (Dependability) & Domain

Functional Requirements (FR)

Define what the system must do: specific services provided, how the system reacts to particular inputs, and how it behaves in specific situations (including what it must not do).

Non-Functional Requirements (NFR)

Define constraints on services or runtime behavior. Failing a single NFR can make the entire system unusable:

  • Product: Usability, Efficiency (performance and space), and Dependability (Reliability, Resilience, Availability, Security, Safety).
  • Organisation: Operational Process Specifications (OPS) and Development Process Specifications (DPS), such as operating procedures, required languages, DBMSs, and development standards.
  • Environmental: Constraints on storage, memory, processing capacity, network speed, and runtime environment.
  • External: Regulatory, legal, ethical, and interoperability constraints.

Domain Requirements

Requirements or constraints derived directly from the application domain rather than specific user wishes—often involving specialized formulas or industry regulations.

Dependability & Availability Metric (Non-Functional Product Requirement) Dependability = {Reliability, Resilience, Availability, Security, Safety}   |   Availability = MTTF ÷ (MTTF + MTTR)

Functional Requirement Decomposition: Account Creation

  1. Register: The system lets a user register with an email address, username, and password.
  2. Confirm: The system sends a confirmation email to verify the account before activation.
  3. Activate: The system activates the account after the user follows the link in that email.

Each step is a distinct functional requirement; the confirmation requirement can be decomposed further into message generation, delivery, and verification behavior.

StageCore Activities & Deliverables

2. Elicitation & AnalysisSoftware engineers work with stakeholders to discover the application domain, required services, and constraints using interviews, observation/ethnography, scenarios, and use cases, then classify and resolve conflicting requirements.
3. Specification (SRS)Translates gathered needs into a structured Software Requirements Specification (SRS) containing both natural-language User Requirements and detailed System Requirements.

7.3 Layman Big-Picture Summary

Plain-English Story

Imagine building a custom house room by room instead of spending two years drawing giant blueprints and hoping the owner likes the final building. First, Requirements Engineering figures out what the owner actually needs—separating big business goals from user wishes and strict technical rules (functional features vs. non-functional constraints like safety and speed). Old models like Waterfall and the V-Model tried to lock every single requirement upfront, which caused heavy paperwork and pain whenever priorities changed. Modern Agile development flips that mindset: teams welcome change by delivering small working pieces fast and talking directly to the customer. Scrum—named after a rugby scrummage where teammates huddle tightly together to push the ball forward as one unit—puts Agile into action. A cross-functional, self-managing team of 10 or fewer people relies on empiricism and lean thinking, practicing three daily habits: keeping work visible (Transparency), checking progress constantly (Inspection), and adjusting immediately when something drifts (Adaptation), backed by five team values (Commitment, Focus, Openness, Respect, and Courage). The Product Owner orders one master wish-list (the Product Backlog, kept clean with DEEP, INVEST, and CTF rules); every 1–4 week Sprint begins with Sprint Planning to build the Sprint Backlog; Developers build the software while syncing for 15 minutes every day (Daily Scrum) as the Scrum Master clears blockers; and at the end of the cycle, they demo a real working Increment to stakeholders in a Sprint Review before improving their teamwork in a Sprint Retrospective.

9.1 The TMT Framework (Theory → Method → Tool)

TMT Causal Hierarchy Theory (Why) —[shapes]→ Method (What & How) —[automated by]→ Tool (Support)
TMT Core Definitions
  • Theory: The underlying scientific, mathematical, or empirical principles explaining why a software engineering approach works (e.g., Empiricism & Lean Thinking, or Structured Upfront Verification).
  • Method: A structured set of procedures, rules, roles, and notations derived from a theory to guide how software is developed (e.g., Scrum, V-Model, Object-Oriented Analysis & Design).
  • Tool: Automated or semi-automated software support that makes applying a method faster, less error-prone, and visible (e.g., Jira, GitHub Projects, UML CASE tools, JUnit).

9.2 Pre-Midterm Software Process Models (Lectures 8 & 9)

  • Generic vs. Tailored Process Models: A generic model (like Waterfall or Incremental) is an abstract template; a tailored process adapts those activities to a specific organization, team size, and risk profile.
  • Four Fundamental Software Process Activities: (1) Software Specification, (2) Software Development, (3) Software Validation, and (4) Software Evolution.

9.3 Staged Delivery Loop (Lecture 8)

Each stage runs a linear requirements–design–development–testing cycle. User feedback returns to requirements as negative feedback, so defects and requested changes can shape the next iteration.

Mid-Syllabus Model Core Mechanism & Key Terms Best Used When (Strengths) Main Limitation
Waterfall & Iterative Waterfall
(Royce, 1970s)		 Linear sequential phases (Requirements → Design → Implementation → Testing → Maintenance); Iterative Waterfall adds backward feedback loops to adjacent phases. Small, stable projects with well-understood, fixed requirements and heavy documentation needs (protects against staff turnover). Rigid to changing requirements; working software and testing happen only near the end.
V-Model
(Verification & Validation)		 Bends Waterfall into a V-shape: left side = specification/design; right side = corresponding testing levels; horizontal links create test cases early at every stage. Safety-critical or regulated systems requiring strict early test planning and zero tester bias. Heavy upfront planning and documentation; struggles when user needs evolve mid-project.
Prototyping
(Throwaway vs. Evolutionary)		 Building an early working model at Low, Medium, or High fidelity. Throwaway/Reference clarifies requirements then is discarded; Evolutionary/Foundation evolves into the final product. Unclear UI/UX or poorly understood user requirements needing rapid visual validation. Throwaway prototypes add upfront cost; evolutionary prototypes risk poor internal architecture if rushed.
Incremental Development
(Staged vs. Parallel)		 Delivers working slices (increments) over time; an increment adds functionality. A decrement removes redundant or unwanted features. Staged IDM groups modules into sequential stages; Parallel / Subsystem Construction overlaps activities or splits subsystems across concurrent teams. Delivering high-priority business value early while reducing the risk of total project failure. Plan-driven incremental still tries to identify all increments upfront before building.
Integration & Configuration
(Reuse-Oriented / COTS)		 Assembles existing tested components (Integration = connecting modules/APIs; Configuration = adapting generic COTS software to user needs). Fast, lower-cost delivery when commercial or open-source components already exist. Requirements must often compromise to fit vendor capabilities; risk of vendor lock-in.

9.5 Reuse and Prototyping Workflows (Lectures 8 & 9)

Integration & Configuration (Reuse)

  1. Requirements specification
  2. Software discovery
  3. Software evaluation
  4. Requirements refinement
  5. Configure the application system

Discovery/evaluation questions: Is the component reliable? What does it cost? How do the available options compare?

Prototyping: Four-Step Flow

  1. Show known functions and their design.
  2. Identify unknown functions.
  3. Validate both known and unknown functions.
  4. Help the project cope with change.

A prototype lets users explore a model early, refine unclear requirements, and give feedback before full production.

Verification vs. Validation

  • Verification: “Are we building the product right?” (Static analysis: inspecting SRS, design, and code without executing it).
  • Validation: “Are we building the right product?” (Dynamic analysis: executing software against real user requirements).

Plan-Driven vs. Evolutionary

  • Plan-Driven (Predictive): Plans all stages upfront assuming stable requirements (Waterfall, V-Model).
  • Evolutionary (Adaptive): Interleaves specification, development, and validation to embrace change (Agile / Scrum).

Q17 • 4 Marks • TMT Connected Synthesis

Explain the TMT (Theory, Method, Tool) relationship in Software Engineering by providing two distinct examples, explaining each component, and showing how they connect to each other. (4 marks)

(a) Stem vs. Preamble

Why this is critical: Directly addresses your professor's feedback—define Theory, Method, and Tool, then build a causal bridge (Theory → shapes Method → automated by Tool) for two distinct examples.

(b) Full-Mark Student Answer

In Software Engineering, TMT (Theory, Method, Tool) describes a three-layer hierarchy where a foundational Theory explains the underlying principles of how software behaves or should be built, a Method translates that theory into a structured set of steps and roles, and a Tool provides automated software support to execute that method efficiently. Rather than existing in isolation, each theory directly dictates the rules of its method, and the tool enforces those rules in daily practice.

We can further understand this connection through two distinct examples. First, in Agile development, the underlying Theory is empiricism and lean thinking, which states that decisions must come from continuous observation while eliminating waste. This theory directly shapes the Method—the Scrum framework—which operationalizes empiricism through time-boxed Sprints, an emergent Product Backlog, and frequent inspection events like the Daily Scrum and Sprint Review. To execute this method smoothly, teams use a Tool such as Jira or a GitHub Kanban Board, which makes the Product Backlog and Sprint Backlog visually transparent and tracks Sprint progress automatically. Second, in plan-driven software engineering, the underlying Theory is structured, predictive engineering and early defect prevention, which holds that thorough upfront specification and verification reduce downstream rework. This theory shapes the Method—the V-Model—which enforces sequential development phases paired with corresponding verification and validation test stages. Teams then rely on Tools such as UML modeling tools (e.g., StarUML) and automated test-management suites to formalize upfront system diagrams and trace every requirement directly to its test case.
